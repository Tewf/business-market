# Against

The evidence against what the front page assumes, and the disagreements. Sources are in [review](review.md). None is deleted to make the subject look stronger.

**Shape:** [against](https://github.com/Tewf/reader-ergonomics-review/blob/c43f2770000a9af5c2aa3cbc6f997da7a7e13b34/shapes/against.md)

## Against "the two sizes usually disagree, and the gap says how far to trust either"

- The one worked pair found shows a gap that is a single premise, whether the product expands the market, and not a measure of trust (Gurley 2014; Damodaran 2014, posts read). Convention, untested.
- No study was found that relates the size of a gap to the error of a later sale (protocol, F26, F27). Not found, in these searches.
- On series of sales, neither method won alone: a split by forecast proportions and a combination of levels did best (Athanasopoulos 2009, abstract, secondary). Measured, on existing series.

## Against "a difference that can be copied is a delay, not a moat"

- Business-specific effects on returns are large and stable, so some advantages last in the data (Rumelt 1991, abstract, secondary). Measured.
- Causal ambiguity, a difference a rival cannot trace to its cause, is argued to block imitation even when the difference is seen (Reed 1990, located only). Convention.
- A moat is not one thing: small advantages added together were found to sustain a lead (Mauboussin 2016, footnote 4, read). Convention.

## Against "a rule for one side of a platform can break the other"

- What the standard model implies depends on how users differ, so no rule holds in general (Weyl 2010, abstract, secondary). Convention, argued from a model.
- The one measured case, magazines, fits the subsidy pattern and says nothing of a rule that breaks a side (Kaiser 2006, abstract, secondary). Measured, one industry.

## Against "a personalised good is a different good in the market"

- Competitive use of purchase data can erode differentiation, so tailoring may make goods more alike (Zhang 2011p, abstract, secondary). Convention, argued from a model.
- The effect of more options on choice is virtually zero on average, so more variants do not by themselves lift demand (Scheibehenne 2010, abstract, secondary). Measured.

## Disagreements

| Point | One side | Other side |
|---|---|---|
| How much industry matters | small stable industry effects (Rumelt 1991) | about 19% of the variance, more in services and trade (McGahan 1997) |
| Which sizing method wins | a split by forecast proportions and a combination did best of five (Athanasopoulos 2009) | the result of the bottom-up comparison on 15,000 series was not read (Dangerfield 1992) |
| Whether choice overload is real | a mean effect of virtually zero (Scheibehenne 2010) | a conceptual review with moderators; result not read (Chernev 2015) |

- A possible reason for the first split is the data: business units of large firms against listed corporations by four-digit industry. Both agree that the business-specific effect is the largest. A hypothesis, not tested here.

## Against the sources themselves

- Seventeen formal sources were read from an abstract and fourteen were only located; every figure from an abstract is secondary.
- The two best-read sizing sources are blogs by an investor in the company they size and by a professor who changed his mind in public; neither is a test.
- The failure page is self-reported by founders and investors, from one producer that sells data.
- The platform and personalisation results are mostly models. Only magazines, bookstores and bicycles are measured, each one industry.
- No source tests a venture's own sizing against its later sales, so Q1 has no measured error to report.
